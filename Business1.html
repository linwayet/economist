Dubai has long positioned itself as a safe haven for international business and wealthy residents in a conflict-prone region. By maintaining political neutrality and providing world-class infrastructure, the emirate attracted investors, businesses, and expatriates seeking stability and economic opportunity. However, Iran's recent military retaliation on February 28th, 2026, directly targeted critical Dubai infrastructure—including the Fairmont hotel, an AWS data center, the international airport, and the port—exposing the city's vulnerability for the first time. This attack has shattered Dubai's carefully cultivated image of safety and immunity from regional conflicts. While immediate panic is limited and authorities are working to restore confidence, businesses are now reassessing political risk through insurance and contingency planning. The fundamental question facing Dubai's leadership is whether the city can retain its attractiveness to global companies, wealthy individuals, and tourists when conflict has literally reached their doorstep. Success depends on swift government action and external factors beyond rulers' control.

第 3 頁
彩色多頁排版主題：奶茶暖調
這一切曾經看起來好得令人難以置信。在一個飽受衝突困擾的地區，杜拜卻呈現出超然物外的樣子。它吸引著富裕且經濟自由的人士來此生活、工作、賺錢，或享受購物和陽光。它成為了一個全球運輸樞紐，連接東西方。它利用財富參與人工智能競賽。所有這一切的關鍵是遠離地區衝突。即使在2023年10月Hamas對以色列的攻擊、隨後的加薩戰爭，或去年伊朗與以色列之間的12天戰鬥，都沒有太大程度地動搖信心。
現在，伊朗對於自2月28日開始的美國和以色列攻擊的報復，已經打擊到了所有這一切的核心——並動搖了杜拜的安全光環。第一天，位於棕櫚島上的Fairmont酒店陷入火海。Amazon Web Services (AWS)數據中心著火了。作為Emirates航空公司的總部——世界上最大的國際航空公司——以及對杜拜旅遊業至關重要的機場，遭到破壞，航班被暫停。Jebel Ali港口是這個酋長國快速增長的港口和轉運樞紐，暫停了營運。碎片落在居民的後院。
到目前為止，杜拜和整個阿聯酋都表現出了韌性；現在還為時過早，無法判斷這場戰爭可能帶來的長期影響。但是，幾天前還是小字體內容的東西，現在已經變成了現實。儘管大多數外籍居民堅守崗位，但有些人正在離開或計劃離開，哪怕只是暫時，通過阿曼或沙烏地阿拉伯，那裡的領空保持開放。美國國務院已建議公民離開該地區。企業也在堅守崗位，但正在制定應急計劃。問題是，曾經推動杜拜成長為全球商業樞紐的人力和資金是否會像以前一樣繼續湧入。
為了吸引人才，杜拜長期以來一直在發放更多長期簽證，並使外國人更容易購買房產。這個酋長國去年的人口增長了5.6%，是2019年以來最快的增長率，達到390萬人。百萬富翁的數量也在快速增長，渴望為他們服務的企業也隨之而來。去年，當時Standard Chartered銀行的首席財務官稱其杜拜業務是其他財富管理中心的「藍圖」。杜拜更廣泛的金融業也在蓬勃發展：在2025年上半年，超過1,000家企業在其金融中心成立，比一年前增加了近三分之一。去年，美國最大的銀行JPMorgan Chase擴展了其本地業務。

科技巨頭也被吸引到杜拜。它是至少18個數據中心的所在地（包括戰爭爆發後遭到襲擊的AWS設施），佔阿聯酋總數的一半。反過來，阿聯酋擁有波斯灣計劃中的數據中心投資的一半以上。阿聯酋人在人工智能上投入了大量資金，包括與Microsoft和BlackRock的合作夥伴關係，價值300億美元的基礎設施投資，以及在OpenAI、Anthropic和xAI中的股份。
雖然現在還處於早期階段，但這些攻擊正在改變企業的計算。很少有企業甚至曾經考慮過阿聯酋與戰爭相關的風險。現在他們正在考慮政治風險保險，這是企業通常為了在新興市場保護自己而購買的政策，諮詢公司Marsh的地緣政治和經濟風險分析主管Christopher Coppock說。這種保險的價格是根據一個國家恐怖主義、戰爭和罷工的風險，以及被保險活動的關鍵程度和確切位置（例如，靠近軍事基地會花費更多）等因素定價的。他說，「從企業的角度來看，所發生的事情是無法改變的。」
任何考慮離開或縮減業務的公司都必須權衡在這個酋長國以外的替代品。一些在香港或新加坡度過疫情期間多年後遷移到杜拜的金融企業可能急於這次快速決定，所以杜拜可能必須迅速採取行動以留住他們。
杜拜統治者盡力灌輸信心。大多數伊朗無人機和導彈已被攔截，很少有恐慌。Carrefour（一個法國連鎖店）和Spinneys（也受歡迎的西方人）等超市表示供應充足。港口運營商DP World迅速恢復了營運。阿聯酋總統Mohammed Bin Zayed在3月2日在杜拜購物中心（一個象徵該市魅力的巨大象徵）出現。
儘管如此，白色煙柱、導彈攔截的爆炸聲和戰鬥機的轟鳴聲在以光彩和魅力聞名的城市中是難以忽視的。充滿空氣的則是令人不適的寂靜。官員們表示杜拜在危機中「樞紐轉向」。它現在採取行動以安撫企業、投資者和人民的速度將是其下一個大考驗。但許多因素超出了統治者的控制。

Business | New calculus needed
The Iran war is a jolt to Dubai’s business model Can the emirate keep people and investors coming?

It seemed too good to be true. In a region plagued by conflict, Dubai had the air of a place apart. It drew the well-heeled and economically footloose, to live, work and make money, or to enjoy shopping and sun. It became a global transport hub, linking east and west. It has used its wealth to join the artificial-intelligence race. Key to it all was keeping out of regional conflicts. Not even Hamas’s attack on Israel in October 2023, the ensuing Gaza war or last year’s 12-day fight between Iran and Israel did much to knock confidence. Now Iran’s retaliation against the American and Israeli attacks that began on February 28th has hit the heart of all this—and shaken Dubai’s halo of safety. The Fairmont, a hotel on the Palm Jumeirah, a swanky man-made property development, was in flames on day one. An Amazon Web Services (aws) data centre caught fire. The airport, home to Emirates, the world’s largest international airline, and essential to Dubai’s tourist trade, was damaged and flights were suspended. Jebel Ali, the emirate’s fast-growing port and transshipment hub, paused operations. Debris fell in residents’ backyards. Dubai, like the rest of the United Arab Emirates (uae), has so far been resilient; it is too soon to tell what the long-term effects of the war might be. But what until a few days ago was the stuff of small print has become reality. Though most foreign residents are sitting tight, some are leaving or planning to, if only for a while, via Oman or Saudi Arabia, where airspace has stayed open. America’s State Department has advised citizens to leave the region. Businesses, too, are staying put, but are making contingency plans. The question is whether the people and money that have fuelled Dubai’s rise as a global business hub will keep coming as they did before. To attract people, Dubai has for some time been issuing more long-term visas and making it easier for foreigners to buy homes. The emirate’s population grew by 5.6% last year, the quickest rate since 2019, to 3.9m. The number of millionaires has been rising fast, too, and businesses eager to serve them have followed. Last year the then chief financial officer of Standard Chartered, a bank, called its Dubai operation a “blueprint” for its other wealth-management centres. Dubai’s broader financial industry is also thriving: in the first half of 2025 more than 1,000 firms set up shop in its financial centre, almost a third more than did so a year before. Last year JPMorgan Chase, America’s biggest bank, expanded its local outpost. Technology giants have also been attracted to Dubai. It is home to at least 18 data centres (including the aws facility struck after war broke out), half the uae’s total. In turn, the uae boasts more than half of the Gulf’s planned data-centre investments. The Emiratis are placing big bets on ai, including partnerships with Microsoft and BlackRock for $30bn-worth of investments in infrastructure, and stakes in Openai, Anthropic and xai. Though it is early days, the attacks are changing companies’ calculations. Few seem to have even thought about war-related risk in the uae before. Now they are considering political-risk insurance, policies that firms usually buy to protect themselves in emerging markets, says Christopher Coppock, head of geopolitical and economic risk analysis at Marsh, a consultancy. Such insurance is priced based on factors such as a country’s risk of terrorism, war and strikes, as well as how critical the activity insured is and exactly where it is (being close to a military base, for instance, will cost more). “From the perspective of businesses, there is no undoing what has happened,” he says. Any companies thinking of leaving, or pulling back, will have to weigh the alternatives to being in the emirate. Some financial firms that moved to Dubai after years of wading through the pandemic in Hong Kong or Singapore may be keen to decide quickly this time, so Dubai may have to act fast to keep them. Dubai’s rulers have done their best to instil confidence. Most Iranian drones and missiles have been intercepted, and there has been little panic. Supermarkets such as Carrefour, a French chain, and Spinneys, also popular with Westerners, say they are well-supplied. dp World, the port operator, quickly resumed operations. The uae’s president, Mohammed Bin Zayed, was out and about in Dubai Mall, a giant symbol of the city’s allure, on March 2nd. Even so, plumes of white smoke, the booms of missile interceptions and the thunder of fighter jets are hard to miss in a city known for its glitz and glamour. So is the contrasting eerie quiet that mostly fills the air. Officials have said Dubai “pivots” well in a crisis. How quickly it now acts to reassure businesses, investors and people is its next big test. But much is out of its rulers’ control. ■

第 1 頁
彩色多頁排版主題：奶茶暖調
Europe faces a trio of interlinked structural weaknesses: an ageing population straining public finances, underdeveloped capital markets, and a scarcity of innovative firms. This article argues that reforming pension systems could simultaneously address all three. Currently, most European pensions operate on the pay-as-you-go (PAYG) model, invented by Germany's Otto von Bismarck in 1889, where current workers fund current retirees. As populations age, this model becomes increasingly unaffordable. Sweden and the Netherlands offer instructive exceptions: by directing contributions into professionally-invested market funds, Sweden has built pension assets worth 110% of GDP and produces the highest number of tech unicorns per capita in the EU. The article calls for three specific reforms: building debt-funded investment buffer funds for PAYG systems; making occupational pensions default-invested into capital markets; and allowing individuals to direct some contributions to personal investment accounts. The resulting short-term fiscal gap, it argues, is worthwhile given the long-run payoff of a more sustainable, market-based pension system. With Europe's tech sector showing signs of life and its €30 trillion ($26 trillion) pension savings severely underinvested, the potential returns from reform have never been higher.

歐洲的退休金制度，從一開始就不是為了把工人變成資本家而設計的。1889年，德國「鐵血宰相」奧托·馮·俾斯麥發明了隨收隨付（pay-as-you-go）制度，由在職工人支付當期退休人員的退休金。其初衷，是「賄賂工人，讓他們把國家視為一種社會機構」。事後看來，工人們卻將每一次退休年齡的提高，都視為對這份承諾的背叛。因此，隨著人口老化，這一制度已承受巨大壓力。工人必須繳納更多退休金，納稅人也需填補剩餘缺口。在德國，今年聯邦預算預計有三分之一將撥付給退休金系統。
與此同時，歐洲的資本市場嚴重發育不良。歐盟股市總市值僅相當於GDP的85%，而美國這一比例高達220%。這對創新至關重要——以市場為基礎的融資，比銀行貸款更適合承擔高風險的研發活動。此外，創投投資者需要深度的資本市場，以便在退出時出售其持股。
然而，有些例外值得借鑒。瑞典建立了緩衝其隨收隨付制度的投資基金。部分退休金繳費不再用於資助當期退休人員，而是轉入個人投資帳戶。其結果是，退休金資產規模達到約6,710億美元，相當於GDP的110%。這絕非偶然——沒有其他歐盟國家的獨角獸企業數量（按人頭計算）比瑞典更多。荷蘭退休金儲蓄者在集體基金中累積了約相當於GDP 145%的資產；自2023年起，這些基金獲准更自由地投資風險性資產。根據摩根士丹利的數據，丹麥和瑞士在擴大市場型職業退休金後的十年內，資產規模增加了約相當於GDP的20%。若將此做法複製到整個歐元區，可釋放逾3兆歐元（約2.58兆美元）的資金。

Europe’s economic problems include a greying population, a lack of innovative firms and puny capital markets. Public pensions weigh more on government budgets with every passing year. But what if Europe could turn those weaknesses into strengths by using its pension savings to boost markets and finance entrepreneurs with long-term capital? Europe’s pensions were not designed to turn workers into capitalists. In 1889 Otto von Bismarck, Germany’s “iron chancellor”, invented the pay-as-you-go system, whereby current workers pay for current pensions. The idea was to “bribe [workers] to regard the state as a social institution”. As it turns out, they see every increase in the retirement age as a betrayal of that promise. The system has therefore come under severe strain as populations have aged. Workers have had to hand over more in contributions, and taxpayers have plugged the remaining shortfall. In Germany a third of the federal budget is projected to be passed on to the pension system this year. The combined value of stockmarkets in the eu is 85% of gdp, compared with 220% of gdp in America. That matters for innovation, because market-based funding is more suitable for risky r&d than bank lending. Moreover, venture-capital investors need a deep capital market into which they can sell their holdings. There are exceptions, however, and they are instructive. Sweden has created funds that buffer its pay-as-you-go system by investing in markets. Some contributions no longer fund today’s pensioners, but go into personal-investment accounts instead. The result is pension assets worth about $671bn, or 110% of gdp. It isn’t an accident that no other eu country has created more unicorns per head. Dutch pension savers have accumulated assets of around 145% of gdp in collective funds; since 2023 these are freer to invest in riskier assets. According to Morgan Stanley, Denmark and Switzerland boosted assets by about 20% of gdp in a decade after expanding market-based occupational pensions. Were that copied across the euro zone, more than €3trn ($2.58trn) would be available. Europeans used to state-run systems will wince at the idea of exposing their future incomes to the markets. And capital-market theorists may add that there will not be much of a pay-off if savers diversify by flocking to America instead. Yet high public-debt burdens mean that relying on state pension promises is no longer as attractive as it once was. And worthy as diversification is, in practice many pension funds will prefer to keep a big chunk of their money at home. Those European governments that do not have high debt-servicing costs should start building debt-funded buffer funds, invested professionally, for their pay-as-you-go systems. Occupational pensions should be deployed into capital markets by default. Savers should be allowed to direct some of their contributions to an individual investment account. The resulting gap in the public-pension system could be filled with wider fiscal deficits in the short term, because the long-run benefit is a more sustainable system that relies less on the public purse. Even with these reforms, the transition to a more market-based system will take time. All the more reason to start now. The European Commission has long sought to stitch together capital markets across the eu. But those efforts will not amount to much if each market remains tiny. Europe’s tech scene is showing signs of life. The potential returns on pension reform have never been higher. ■
